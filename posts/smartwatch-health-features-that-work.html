Smartwatch Health Features: What Actually Works (Backed by Research)

By Ryan Carter10 min read
The numbers on your wrist are worth a look. Just know which ones deserve a second look.

More than a third of American adults now wear a smartwatch. A Pew Research survey from February 2026 put the number at 37 percent, and the Rock Health 2025 survey of 8,000 adults found 46 percent own some kind of wearable, nearly four times the share from a decade earlier. The little computer on your wrist has gone from gadget to daily habit.

But here is the question almost nobody asks before buying one: do the health features actually work? Your watch claims to measure your heart rate, take an ECG, estimate your blood oxygen, score your sleep, and count your steps. Some of these readings rest on large clinical studies. Others are educated guesses wearing a lab coat. This guide goes through each major smartwatch health feature, explains what the research really says, and tells you how much to trust the number on your wrist.

Heart rate: the feature that mostly delivers

Optical heart-rate sensors shine green light into your wrist and measure how much bounces back as blood pulses through. It sounds flimsy, but at rest it is remarkably good. A 2020 systematic review by Zhang and colleagues found wrist sensors typically landed within one to three beats per minute of reference devices during rest and sleep. For checking your resting heart rate in the morning or watching it settle after a walk, the number is solid.

Exercise is where things get messier. The same review found errors widened during resistance training and cycling. A 2026 study in the journal Sensors tested several watches against a Polar H10 chest strap and found error rates of just 1.6 to 2.9 percent at rest, but 12 to 25 percent during burpees, the kind of explosive arm movement that shakes the sensor against the skin.

The gold standard remains the chest strap. In research led by Dr. Marc Gillinov at the Cleveland Clinic and published in JAMA Cardiology in 2019, the Polar H7 chest strap agreed with clinical ECG measurements at 0.99 on a correlation scale where 1.0 is perfect. The best wrist device in that study, the Apple Watch, scored 0.80, while the weakest scored 0.52. If you are training seriously for a race, a fifty-dollar chest strap still beats a four-hundred-dollar watch. For everyday awareness of your resting and active heart rate, the wrist sensor is plenty.

ECG and irregular rhythm alerts: the best-validated feature on your wrist

The most scientifically validated health feature in any smartwatch is the ECG app. You place your finger on the crown, hold still for thirty seconds, and the watch records a single-lead electrocardiogram that can flag atrial fibrillation, the most common heart rhythm disorder.

This is not a wellness gimmick hiding behind a disclaimer. The Apple Watch ECG app received FDA De Novo clearance in September 2018, a pathway reserved for novel devices with no existing equivalent. Samsung followed with FDA clearance for the Galaxy Watch 3 in August 2020, and Fitbit earned its own clearance later that year. These are regulated medical features, not marketing claims.

The evidence base is unusually large. The Apple Heart Study, published in the New England Journal of Medicine in 2019, enrolled 419,297 participants, making it one of the largest cardiovascular studies ever run. Just over half a percent of participants received an irregular-pulse notification, and when those people wore a clinical ECG patch for comparison, 84 percent of the notifications corresponded to atrial fibrillation. Among participants over 65, the notification rate was 3.2 percent, which makes sense because AFib risk rises sharply with age.

Meta-analyses since then have confirmed the accuracy. A 2025 analysis in JACC: Advances pooling 11 studies and 4,241 participants found the Apple Watch ECG detected atrial fibrillation with 94.8 percent sensitivity and 95.0 percent specificity. A second, larger meta-analysis from October 2025 covering 26 studies and 17,379 patients found pooled sensitivity of 94.8 percent and specificity of 96.1 percent, with Samsung's watches scoring slightly higher on sensitivity at 96.6 percent.

One honest caveat: the watch is good at confirming AFib when you take a reading yourself, but less impressive at catching it passively in the background. A prospective study at the Cebu Heart Institute compared the Apple Watch against 24-hour Holter monitors in 140 patients and found background notifications caught only 21 percent of AFib episodes, although a manually triggered ECG was essentially perfect. If your watch alerts you about an irregular rhythm, take it seriously and take a manual ECG, but do not assume that silence means your heart is flawless.

Blood oxygen: a useful trend line, not a diagnosis

Blood oxygen saturation, or SpO2, tells you what percentage of your blood is carrying oxygen. Healthy readings sit between 95 and 100 percent. Watches estimate it with red and infrared light, and the accuracy is decent but clearly a step below heart rate.

In a Duke University study published in PLOS Digital Health, researchers tested five popular devices against a clinical-grade pulse oximeter in 49 adults. The Apple Watch Series 7 came out best with an average error of 2.2 percent, while the Garmin Venu 2s trailed at 5.8 percent. The Apple Watch also succeeded in getting a reading 89 percent of the time, versus just 70 percent for the Withings ScanWatch.

There is one limitation worth knowing. In February 2021, the FDA issued a safety communication noting that pulse oximeters, both the finger-clip kind used in hospitals and wrist sensors, can be less accurate in people with darker skin pigmentation. A 2022 meta-analysis found overestimation of roughly 1.1 to 1.5 percent in patients with high skin pigmentation. Treat your watch's SpO2 as a trend to watch over time rather than a precise number on any given morning. A slow drift downward across weeks is meaningful. A single reading of 94 percent after a restless night is not a reason to panic.

Sleep tracking: great at "asleep or awake," fuzzy on the details

If you wear your watch to bed, it will tell you how long you slept and break the night into light, deep, and REM stages. The first part is trustworthy. The second part deserves skepticism.

Researchers at Brigham and Women's Hospital tested the Oura Ring, Fitbit Sense 2, and Apple Watch Series 8 against polysomnography, the wired-up lab test that is the gold standard for sleep measurement, in 35 adults. Every device distinguished sleep from wakefulness with at least 95 percent sensitivity. But when it came to identifying sleep stages, accuracy fell to between 50 and 86 percent. The Apple Watch underestimated deep sleep by 43 minutes and overestimated light sleep by 45 minutes on average.

The practical advice: trust your watch's total sleep time and its wake-up time, and treat the colorful stage breakdown as an estimate. If your watch says you got six and a half hours, you probably got about six and a half hours. If it says you got 38 minutes of deep sleep, the real number could be meaningfully different in either direction.

Sleep apnea detection is a newer and more serious application. Apple's sleep apnea notification feature, cleared by the FDA in September 2024, was validated in 1,448 people: it caught 66 percent of moderate-to-severe cases while correctly clearing 98.5 percent of people without the condition. Samsung's version, cleared in February 2024, reported 83 percent sensitivity and 91 percent specificity in its FDA filing documents. These are screening tools, not diagnoses, but for a condition where the large majority of cases go undiagnosed, a nudge toward a real sleep study has genuine value.

Step counting: the most accurate thing your watch does

After all that nuance, here is something simple: step counting is the most accurate health metric on your wrist. A 2024 King's College London study measured watches against a calibrated meter wheel during walking tests and found step-count errors of just 0.9 percent for Garmin and 6.8 percent for Fitbit. The commonly accepted threshold for "accurate enough" in research is 10 percent, so both clear it comfortably.

The accuracy holds for normal walking and breaks down at the edges. A 2026 study in PeerJ found the Apple Watch undercounted steps by 16 percent during very slow walking and by 36 percent when participants used a rolling walker, because the shuffling gait barely registers on the accelerometer. For the vast majority of owners walking at a normal pace, though, the daily step total is the number on your watch you can trust most.

Blood pressure: promising, but not there yet

Blood pressure is the next frontier, and the honest summary is that watches are not ready to replace the cuff. Apple's hypertension notification feature, FDA-cleared in 2025 for the Apple Watch Series 11 and Ultra 3, does not measure your blood pressure at all. It analyzes subtle changes in the optical heart sensor's signal over 30 days and alerts you if the pattern suggests hypertension. In a validation study led by researchers at the University of Pennsylvania and published as a JAMA research letter, the feature alerted 41 percent of wearers who had undiagnosed hypertension, which means it missed 59 percent, while falsely alerting about 8 percent of people with normal blood pressure. Apple itself says the feature is meant to prompt a checkup, not to diagnose.

Samsung takes a different approach with actual blood pressure estimation on the Galaxy Watch, but that feature is not FDA-cleared for use in the United States, which tells you something about where regulators think the technology stands. For a measurement where a few points can determine your diagnosis, the technology is not there yet. Keep the arm cuff for now.

How to use all this without driving yourself crazy

Knowing the accuracy of each sensor changes how you should read your data. Use heart rate and step counts as daily feedback you can act on. Treat ECG readings as genuinely useful medical information, and take any irregular-rhythm alert seriously enough to follow up with your doctor. Read blood oxygen and sleep stages as trends across weeks, not verdicts on any single day. And ignore any blood pressure estimate that did not come from a cuff.

The deeper pattern in the research is encouraging. A 2023 survey by Huawei and IPSOS found 87 percent of smartwatch owners had adopted new healthy habits since buying one, and 81 percent said they trusted their device's health data. The Rock Health 2025 survey found 83 percent of wearable owners wear their device at least five days a week. The watches are not perfect instruments, but they are good enough to change behavior, and behavior is what actually improves health.

One more thing worth knowing: accuracy is only half the buying decision. Battery life, price, and whether the watch works with your phone matter just as much in daily life. The bottom line

ECG and heart rate: genuinely good. Step counts: excellent. Sleep duration: reliable, sleep stages: approximate. Blood oxygen: useful for trends. Blood pressure: not ready yet. None of these features replaces a doctor, but the best of them have now been tested on hundreds of thousands of people and published in major medical journals. That is a long way from where wearables were a decade ago, when roughly a third of owners abandoned their device within six months.
