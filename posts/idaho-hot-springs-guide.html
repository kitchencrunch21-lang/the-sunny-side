Idaho Hot Springs: A Slow Soak Through America's Best Soaking State

By Ryan Carter9 min read
Idaho rewards the slow traveler.

Idaho does not advertise itself loudly. It does not need to. The state holds 130 soakable hot springs, more than any other state in America, out of roughly 340 geothermal features in total. That is the official count from Visit Idaho, the state tourism office, and it means you could soak in a different Idaho spring every weekend for two and a half years and still not repeat one.

What makes Idaho special is not just the count. It is the range. Within a few hours of Boise you can find a free riverside pool you reach by trail, a historic resort you reach by snowmobile, and a state-run hot pool complex in a town named after the thing itself. This guide walks through the springs worth knowing, what each one costs, when to go, and how to plan a slow loop through them.

Why Idaho wins the hot springs map

Idaho sits on the Yellowstone hotspot's western edge, and the geology shows. Hot water rises along fault lines across the state, from the Sawtooth Mountains to the Snake River Plain, and people have been soaking in it for centuries. The Shoshone-Bannock peoples used the springs long before resorts existed, and the town of Lava Hot Springs grew up around pools that were already famous when the railroad arrived.

The practical result is variety no other state matches. Some springs are wild pools beside a river, free and open to anyone willing to walk. Others are developed resorts with changing rooms, food, and lodging. A few are so remote that reaching them is half the trip. You can build an entire vacation around them, or you can stop at one on the way to somewhere else. Either way, Idaho rewards the slow traveler.

The famous ones, from free pools to full resorts

Goldbug Hot Springs, about 23 miles south of Salmon on Highway 93, is the postcard spring. A 3.6-mile round-trip hike climbs 895 to 1,350 feet (sources disagree on the gain) to six waterfall-fed rock pools terraced down a hillside. The source runs about 113 degrees Fahrenheit and the pools cool to between 90 and 105 as the water flows down, so you pick your temperature by picking your pool. It is free, on public land, and the parking lot fills early on summer weekends, so arrive before 8:30 in the morning if you want a quiet soak.

Kirkham Hot Springs, four or five miles east of Lowman on Highway 21, is the easiest wild soak in the state. A short walk from the car park brings you to a series of pools and small waterfalls on the South Fork of the Payette River, fed by a source of about 120 degrees. The pools settle between 95 and 110, and you can cool off in the river between soaks. Soaking is free, though parking costs about five dollars a day, and the campground has been closed indefinitely. Come on a weekday if you can.

Burgdorf Hot Springs, thirty miles north of McCall, is the opposite of Kirkham: a historic resort in a near-ghost town, where the Harris family has run the springs since 1923 and Fred Burgdorf settled the site in 1870. Three log pools sit at 100 to 113 degrees, fed by sources of about 113. Admission is 20 dollars for adults and 15 for children and seniors, plus tax, and reservations are required. It is open six days a week, closed Wednesdays, reachable by car from June to October and by snowmobile only from December to April. There is no cell service and, in the older descriptions, no electricity. That is the point.

Lava Hot Springs is the town that named itself after the water. The state-run foundation operates five outdoor pools at 102 to 112 degrees, open 363 days a year. Admission is 8 dollars Monday to Thursday and 12 dollars Friday to Sunday and holidays for ages 12 to 59, with lower rates for children and seniors. The water contains bicarbonate, sodium, chloride, sulfate, calcium, magnesium, and potassium, with a pH of about 5.2, and the foundation notes there are no chemicals, no sulfur, and no odor. It is the easiest Idaho soak for a first-timer: drive in, pay, change, and float.

Miracle Hot Springs near Buhl, about 40 minutes from Twin Falls, is a developed resort with four outdoor pools and more than twenty private pools. The source runs 140 degrees, cooled by pipes in Salmon Falls Creek to a soaking range of 96 to 106. The water has a pH of 9.6 and is listed as containing magnesium, calcium, sodium, potassium, and zinc, with no chlorine and pools drained and cleaned daily. General admission starts around 14 dollars for adults, reservations are required, and the resort is closed on Sundays. Do not confuse it with the Miracle Hot Springs in California, which closed indefinitely in 2024 after two deaths. Different state, different spring.

The Springs in Idaho City, about an hour north of Boise on Highway 21, is the upscale option: an 80-acre resort with a large main pool, a hot tub at about 104 degrees, a steam room, and private tubs. A general soak costs 30 dollars for adults and 27 for children for two and a half hours, reservation only, and private tubs run 70 dollars an hour for two adults. It is open Thursday to Monday, closed Tuesday and Wednesday, with adults-only hours on Monday, Thursday, and Friday. Highway 21 construction at Mores Creek Bridge may add delays into 2027.

Jerry Johnson Hot Springs, off Highway 12 near Lolo Pass, is the free hike-in classic on the Lochsa River side. Cross the suspension bridge over the river, walk about a mile and a half along Warm Springs Creek, and you reach pools at 100 to 104 degrees, with source pools running up to 115. Day use runs 6 a.m. to 8 p.m., no camping at the springs, and the trail is icy in winter, so bring traction devices.

Free soak or resort day?

The choice comes down to what you want the day to feel like. Free natural springs like Goldbug, Kirkham, Jerry Johnson, Sunbeam, and Boat Box cost nothing but effort: a hike, a dirt road, a river crossing, and no facilities beyond a parking lot and your own towel. You trade comfort for solitude and scenery, and you share the pool with whoever else made the same effort.

Developed springs like Lava, Miracle, The Springs, Burgdorf, and Gold Fork charge admission and give you changing rooms, showers, food, and a place to sit that is not a rock. Gold Fork, near Donnelly, runs 15 dollars a person, cash only, for six terraced pools at 80 to 104 degrees. For a family trip or a first soak, the resort is the smarter start. For a quiet morning alone in the mountains, the free pool wins.

When to go

June through October is the most reliable window for road access and stable pool levels. Spring runoff from mid-March to June can flood riverside pools, cool them with snowmelt, and wash out access roads. Winter is the iconic season, soaking in falling snow with steam rising around you, and Lava, Miracle, Kirkham, and Burgdorf all stay open through it, but trails turn icy, campgrounds close, and the hike back to the car in wet clothes is the real risk. Whatever the season, early mornings and weekdays are consistently quieter than weekend afternoons.

The Idaho Hot Springs Loop

There is no official state-designated Idaho Hot Springs Loop byway, despite the name appearing in guidebooks. Two informal routes share the name. The driving version is a roughly 300-mile, one-to-two-day loop from Boise: north on Highway 21, the Ponderosa Pine Scenic Byway, past Pine Flats and Kirkham to Stanley, then back through Sun Valley and Ketchum on Highway 75, the Sawtooth Scenic Byway, stopping at Sunbeam, Boat Box, and Bonneville along the way. The cycling version is a different beast entirely: a 500-mile bikepacking loop from Boise through the Sawtooth and Salmon River mountains, visiting around fifty springs over seven to fourteen days, with a shorter 250-mile option.

Before you set out, it helps to see the whole picture at once. This idaho hot springs map shows where each spring sits along the route, so you can plan stops, driving times, and which pools are free and which need reservations.

The rules that keep the pools clean

There is no single statewide rulebook for Idaho hot springs. Rules are set site by site by the Forest Service, the Bureau of Land Management, or the resort. But the pattern is consistent. Visit Idaho asks visitors to leave every spring as they found it or better, pack out all waste, and never add anything to the water, including sunscreen or lotion on your skin, because natural pools have no drains and no filters. Forest Service sites typically ban glass containers, alcohol, soap and shampoo, and pets, and require clothing at developed sites. Miracle bans alcohol, smoking, and pets. The Springs has zero tolerance for bringing your own alcohol. The details vary. The spirit does not: the next soaker deserves the same clean water you found.

A note on safety before you get in

Natural springs are not hot tubs, and the numbers on the signs are not thermostat settings. Sources in Idaho run 113 to 140 degrees and higher, hot enough to scald, so always test the water with your hand before stepping in and choose a cooler lower pool if the top one is too hot. The often-quoted 104-degree maximum and 15-minute limit come from CDC and CPSC guidance for hot tubs, not from any rule for natural springs. The CDC rule that does apply to hot springs is simpler and more important: always keep your head above the water, because of the rare brain-eating amoeba Naegleria fowleri. The CDC counted 180 US cases from 1937 to 2025, almost all fatal, mostly from warm lakes rather than springs, but the precaution is the same everywhere. Never dive in, never stir up sediment, and never put your head under.

People come to Idaho's springs for the scenery, but they stay in the water for how it makes them feel. The research on hot springs and stress relief explains what warm water actually does to your stress hormones, your sleep, and your nervous system, and why the calm you feel after a soak is real, measurable, and worth planning a trip around.
