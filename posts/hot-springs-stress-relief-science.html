Hot Springs and Stress: What Science Actually Says About Soaking to Unwind

By Ryan Carter9 min read
The calm arrives in the recovery afterwards.

You know the feeling before you can explain it. You lower yourself into hot water, your shoulders drop, your breathing slows, and the day loosens its grip. It feels like stress leaving the body. The question is whether that feeling is real in any measurable sense, or just a pleasant story we tell ourselves about warm water.

The answer, after reading the clinical research, is more interesting than either extreme. Warm-water soaking does move stress markers in measurable ways, but the effects are short-term, the studies are small, and the calm you feel owes more to heat and buoyancy than to the minerals in the water. Here is what the science actually says, study by study, so you can soak with your eyes open.

What happens to stress hormones in hot water

The stress hormone most often measured in soaking research is cortisol, the hormone your body releases when it senses threat or demand. Three studies are worth knowing by name.

The cleanest experiment comes from Japan. In 2018, Goto and colleagues ran a randomized trial with 38 participants: two weeks of whole-body immersion in 40-degree Celsius water for ten minutes a day, compared with two weeks of shower-only bathing. Stress scores on a visual scale fell from 49.0 to 38.7, a drop of 10.2 points, and mood scores for tension, anger, and depression all improved significantly. General health, mental health, and social functioning improved too. The authors attributed the effect to heat widening blood vessels and increasing blood flow, not to anything dissolved in the water.

The largest recent trial comes from Lithuania. Rapoliene and colleagues ran a 2023 randomized trial at six medical spa centers with about 400 participants, all reporting stress above 3 on a 10-point scale, for one to two weeks of balneotherapy. Distress fell by 1 to 3.5 points on the scale, wellbeing rose by up to 3 points, and salivary cortisol fell by 0.9 units after the winter treatment. Interestingly, winter sessions worked better than summer ones, possibly because the contrast between cold air and hot water makes the experience more vivid.

The most honest summary comes from a 2024 meta-analysis by Antonelli and colleagues, which pooled 17 studies and 765 participants. It found a short-term cortisol fall in healthy people and in people under high stress, but the pooled effect size was small and not statistically significant overall. In elderly and rheumatic patients, cortisol actually rose during treatment, which the authors interpreted as a useful, hormetic stress response that reduced inflammation and improved pain and function. The takeaway is not that soaking fails. It is that the effect is real but modest, and it varies by who you are.

The calm comes after, not during

There is a counter-intuitive detail in the physiology that explains a lot about how a soak feels. A 2001 study by Nagasawa and colleagues immersed nine young men and nine elderly men in 40-degree water for ten minutes while measuring heart-rate variability. During immersion, the parasympathetic "rest and digest" signal was actually suppressed in the young men, while blood pressure fell and heart rate rose. The shift toward calm was clearest in recovery, after getting out, when the body cooled down and the nervous system rebalanced.

That timing matters for safety as well as for mood. The same study warns that decreased sympathetic tone a few minutes after immersion can cause blood pressure to drop enough to make you faint, which is how people drown in hot water. The practical advice follows directly from the physiology: soak for ten to fifteen minutes, get out slowly, sit on the edge for a minute before standing, and do not soak alone if you have low blood pressure or a heart condition.

Soaking for better sleep

The strongest evidence for a hot springs benefit may be the one that has nothing to do with springs at all. A 2019 meta-analysis by Haghayegh and colleagues reviewed 17 trials of passive body heating, warm baths, showers, and foot baths, and found that water at 40 to 42.5 degrees Celsius, taken one to two hours before bed for as little as ten minutes, was associated with about ten minutes shorter time to fall asleep, plus better subjective sleep quality and efficiency.

The mechanism is elegant. Warming the skin sends blood to your hands and feet, and as that blood radiates heat, your core temperature falls faster. It is the fall in core temperature, not the warmth itself, that signals sleep. That is why the timing matters: soak too close to bed and you are still warm; soak ninety minutes before and your body is cooling right as you lie down. A hot springs visit in the late afternoon, followed by dinner and an early night, is close to the ideal protocol the researchers describe.

Is it the minerals or just the heat?

Resort marketing loves minerals. Sulfur for skin, magnesium for muscles, lithium for mood. The evidence is thinner than the brochures suggest, and the honest summary is that heat, buoyancy, and water pressure explain most of what soaking does. Warm water dilates blood vessels, buoyancy unloads joints, and hydrostatic pressure gently squeezes the body. Those three mechanisms are well established. Mineral absorption through skin is largely unproven.

Sulfur has the best case of any mineral. In spring water it exists largely as hydrogen sulfide, which can pass through skin and acts as a signaling molecule for blood vessel dilation and inflammation. Thermal mineral water baths improved psoriasis and eczema symptoms in all eight trials in one systematic review. Magnesium, by contrast, has no supporting data for absorption through skin according to a 2017 review in the journal Nutrients, despite being printed on countless spa menus. Lithium at hot-spring concentrations has never been tested in a randomized trial for mood, and the claims are marketing, not evidence.

That does not make mineral content meaningless. The springs in Idaho list their minerals as a matter of composition: Lava Hot Springs publishes bicarbonate, sodium, chloride, sulfate, calcium, magnesium, and potassium figures, and Miracle Hot Springs lists magnesium, calcium, sodium, potassium, and zinc. Read those numbers as what is in the water, not as a promise of what it will do to you. The heat is proven. The minerals are plausible but largely unproven.

Your heart on hot water

During a soak, blood vessels dilate, blood pressure falls, and heart rate rises to compensate, roughly like light exercise. For most healthy adults that is fine, even beneficial. For people with heart disease, the advice is more careful. The Cleveland Clinic advises heart patients to limit soaks to five to ten minutes, keep the temperature moderate, stay hydrated, and talk to their doctor first, because sudden or extended immersion can divert blood from the core and strain a weakened heart. Mayo Clinic research found that fifteen minutes in 40-degree water created less cardiovascular stress than moderate cycling in men with stable heart disease, which is reassuring, but it is not a green light to soak for an hour.

Pregnancy deserves a clear note. The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists says it is best not to use a hot tub or sauna early in pregnancy, and the CDC advises talking to a healthcare provider first. The Consumer Product Safety Commission recommends a 100-degree maximum in pregnancy and warns that thermostats can be off by up to four degrees. The evidence behind the caution is a 1992 study in JAMA of 23,491 women, which linked hot-tub use in early pregnancy to about 2.8 times the risk of neural tube defects. There is no official hot-springs-specific pregnancy rule, but natural springs add untreated water and no thermostat to the same risks, so the hot-tub guidance is the one to follow.

The safety fine print, honestly stated

Two numbers you will see everywhere need correcting. The 104-degree Fahrenheit maximum and the 15-minute limit are not federal rules for hot springs. The 104-degree cap comes from CDC and CPSC guidance for hot tubs, and the 15-minute limit comes from New Jersey and Illinois public-spa rules plus Cleveland Clinic advice. Many Idaho sources run 113 to 140 degrees at the source, so the hot-tub numbers cannot be applied literally to natural pools. The safe approach is simpler: test the water before getting in, choose a cooler lower pool, and get out when you feel dizzy, drowsy, or too hot.

The CDC rule that does apply to hot springs is about your head. Always keep your head above the water in hot springs and other naturally hot water, hold your nose if you jump in, and do not stir up sediment in shallow warm water. The reason is Naegleria fowleri, the brain-eating amoeba. The CDC counted 180 US cases from 1937 to 2025, ranging from zero to eight per year, with more than 97 percent fatal. Most cases come from warm lakes rather than springs, and the CDC publishes no breakdown of how many of the 180 were linked specifically to hot springs, so no one should invent one. The precaution costs nothing: head up, nose held, no diving.

Winter soaking adds one more risk that has nothing to do with the water. Wet skin, wind, and sub-freezing air cool the body fast after a hot soak, and hypothermia can begin when core temperature falls below 95 degrees, which the CDC notes can happen even in air above 40 degrees if a person is wet and chilled. Bring a warm robe, dry clothes, a hat and gloves, and dress immediately after getting out. Do not linger wet on an icy trail.

How to soak for calm, in practice

Put the research together and the routine writes itself. Soak for ten to fifteen minutes in water around 100 to 104 degrees, ideally in the late afternoon or early evening, about ninety minutes before bed if sleep is the goal. Get out slowly, cool down, hydrate, and let the calm arrive in the recovery afterwards rather than chasing it during the soak. Do not drink alcohol in the water, do not put your head under, and do not treat the soak as a medical treatment. It is a habit that supports rest, not a cure for anything.
